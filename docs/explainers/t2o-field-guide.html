Thermal → visible translation, detection in the loop

What we count, how we train, and where alignment bites

The pipeline is one line long. A thermal frame goes through a translator, and the result is read by a detector that was only ever trained on visible photos. Everything below is about checking that this line is honest.

Why bother: the same visible detector scores 0.9213 mAP@50 on the real visible photos of the 153 validation pairs, and 0.1887 when you show it the raw thermal frames. The translator's job is to close that gap without making things up.

Five words that get tangled

Hallucination
The umbrella word. The translator paints in an object that is not there, or paints out one that is. We measure it with the two rates below.
Invented object
A box the judge finds on the translation that matches nothing in the ground truth. Its share is the false-object rate (insertion).
Erased object
A ground-truth box the judge cannot find on the translation. Its share is the missed-object rate (deletion).
Faithfulness
The success criterion built from those rates: the loop may not invent or erase more than the no-loop translator, within a margin fixed in advance.
Stability
The result repeats across random seeds: at least 3 seeds, a sign-flip significance test, and no seed that collapses.
PART 1

Counting invented and erased objects

Every faithfulness number starts the same way. A judge detector looks at the translated image and returns boxes. We then pair those boxes with the hand-drawn ground-truth boxes, one by one. Anything left unpaired is a failure: an unpaired prediction is an invented object, an unpaired ground-truth box is an erased one.

The judge is never the detector used during training. If the detector that pushed the translator also graded it, a good score would only show that the translator learned to please that one detector. That is reward hacking, and a separate judge is how we rule it out. The first judge was a yolo11s. The final verdicts use RT-DETR-l, a different family of detector entirely.

    The three rates, in words

    False-object rate = unmatched predictions ÷ all predictions on the translation. Of everything the judge saw, how much was not really there.

    Missed-object rate = unmatched ground-truth boxes ÷ all ground-truth boxes. Of everything really there, how much the judge could not find.

    Detection consistency uses no labels at all. The judge looks at the real visible photo, then at the translation, and we ask what share of its visible-photo boxes it finds again on the translation. Tick the checkbox above to see those blue boxes. This is useful because labels can be wrong. Consistency only asks whether the translation makes the judge behave like the real photo does.

    All three go through the same matcher at the same IoU, so they are comparable to each other and across translators. Counts are summed over the whole validation split first, then divided. The per-class versions (Fuse, Switch, Pole, Transformer) come from the same matches split by class. They add up exactly to the pooled counts.

    Completion rate: a trap for invention

    The rates above can only catch an invented object where nothing was expected. To test invention head-on, the cascade gate erases fuses and switches from the thermal input by inpainting, translates the erased frame, and asks the judge whether it finds them anyway, same class, IoU ≥ 0.5. Any it finds were put back with no evidence in the input.

    Measured: 0 of 234 erased objects came back, on all nine runs.

    The Hallucination Index is parked

    The plan names an adapted Hallucination Index (from a MICCAI 2024 paper). It compares the spread of many random samples per input against a hallucination-free reference. Our translators are deterministic: one input, one output. With nothing to sample, the metric does not apply yet, so it is deferred rather than faked.

    Stability: does it repeat across seeds?

    A single training run is one draw of luck: the random initial weights, the batch order, the augmentation. So every arm runs with several seeds, and the loop and its control share each seed. Seed 3 of the loop is compared with seed 3 of the control. That gives one paired difference per seed.

    The test is the exact sign-flip test. If the loop did nothing, each paired difference would be as likely to be negative as positive. So we flip every combination of signs, recompute the mean each time, and ask how often a mean at least as extreme as ours appears by chance. With 6 seeds there are 2⁶ = 64 sign patterns. Even when all six differences are positive, two of the 64 patterns (all +, all −) are as extreme, so the smallest possible p is 2/64 = 0.031. With 3 seeds the smallest p is 2/8 = 0.25. This is why 6 seeds is the minimum for p < 0.05.

    Collapse is the other half of stability. Sometimes a seed's translator falls apart and the judge finds almost nothing, under 0.02 mAP@50. A method that only works on lucky seeds is not stable, so collapsed seeds are counted and reported, never dropped.

    Faithfulness: the pass rule

    The criterion does not ask for zero hallucination. It asks that the loop be no worse than the no-loop translator, within a margin. The margin for each dataset and each rate was fixed before the verdict was read: the control arm's own seed-to-seed spread under the RT-DETR judge. The rule is then a picture. Take the 95% interval of the paired loop − control difference. For the false- and missed-object rates, the whole interval must sit below +margin. For consistency, which you want high, it must sit above −margin.

    The rule applied to the custom set

    RT-DETR judge, stage 3, record 033

    Turbo's point estimate is tiny (+0.0040), but with only 3 seeds its interval reaches +0.0538, past its margin of 0.0315. Under the rule that is a fail, and it is reported as one. Intervals this small are, if anything, too narrow, so a fail is robust while a pass is the weaker statement.

    PART 2

    How the loop trains the translator

    The translator learns from two teachers at once. The first is the real visible photo: look like this. That is the usual image-translation loss, a mix of pixel difference (L2), perceptual difference (LPIPS) and a GAN critic. The second is a frozen visible detector: be readable by me. We run it on the translated image, compute its normal detection loss against the boxes, and push that loss back through the detector into the translator's weights.

    loss = L2 + LPIPS + GAN + grad_scale × wstage × detection_loss

    The detector is frozen: its weights never change. Only the gradient passes through it. So the translator has to move toward images this fixed detector can already read.

    Why a control arm makes the result mean something

    The control is the same config file with task_weights: [0, 0, 0, 0]. It runs the same four stages, 400 epochs in total, with the same exports and fine-tunes. The only difference is that the detection term never switches on. At weight 0 the frozen detector is not even built, so nothing about it can leak into the control.

    Stage 0 is weight 0 in both arms, so the two arms are identical there. Any loop − control gap at stage 0 is pure seed noise. That gives a free null check inside every experiment: a stage-3 gap is only convincing if it is clearly bigger than the stage-0 gap.

    Three detectors, never mixed up

    In the loop · teacher

    Frozen visible detector

    yolo11n, trained on visible photos. Supplies the detection gradient during stages 1–3. Never updated, never re-pointed at a fine-tuned checkpoint.

    Reference · judge

    Independent judge

    yolo11s, then out-of-family RT-DETR-l. Trained separately on visible photos, never on anything this project made. Scores zero-shot mAP and every faithfulness rate.

    PART 3

    Registration: where alignment enters, and what it costs

    Registration lines up the thermal and visible images of the same scene, so that a pixel in one is the same point in the world as the pixel in the other. The two cameras sit side by side and are never perfectly aligned. Registering them is the sister project's problem. Here it is an input, and its error is one of the axes the paper maps.

    Four places it enters this project

    1. The thermal labels themselves. On the custom set, boxes were drawn on the visible photo and moved onto the thermal frame by the sister project's registration. Every thermal box carries that registration error, including the validation boxes everything is scored against.
    2. Training the translator. The pixel losses compare the output with the visible target pixel by pixel. If the target is shifted, the translator is taught to paint things in the wrong place, or to blur them to hedge. The training boxes come from the same misaligned frame.
    3. The competing route. Label transfer, which moves boxes from visible to thermal and trains a thermal detector on them, is the registration route. Its quality is the registration's quality.
    4. Not at use time. A trained translator needs only a thermal frame. No visible photo, no registration. This is the whole pitch for thermal-only surveys.

    Label-transfer route

    1. Take registered thermal–visible pairs.
    2. Draw boxes on visible, warp them onto thermal.
    3. Train a detector on thermal with those boxes.

    Needs registration for every image you want labelled. The error goes straight into the labels.

    Translation route

    1. Train the translator once on registered pairs.
    2. Translate any thermal frame to RGB.
    3. Run any visible-trained detector on it.

    Needs registration only for the training pairs. The error goes into the training target. It is an open question how much of it survives into the output.

    A box that drops under IoU 0.5 is scored exactly like the shifted switch in Part 1: one erased object and one invented object, though nothing was hallucinated. Small parts lose overlap first, and fastest far from the image centre (the outer fuse). Long thin parts (the pole) come next. These are exactly the fuses and switches the paper cares most about.

    The registration-error axis experiment

    Goal C2 asks when translation pays, and one axis is registration error: how fast each route degrades as alignment gets worse. The experiment injects a known misalignment into the training pairs at 0, 3, 6, 12 and 24 px, on the custom set and on FLIR. Validation images and labels are never touched, so every level is scored on the same frozen boxes.

    Label transfer gets 3 seeds per level, since each is a ten-minute detector fine-tune. Translation gets one pix2pix loop run per level, about 10.5 GPU-hours each. The aligned 3-seed runs already on the server serve as the custom set's zero point. The result is two curves per dataset: mAP@50 against misalignment, one for each route.
